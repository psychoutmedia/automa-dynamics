Measurement report · COLLAPSE · September 2026

The swap test is cosine

I built a quantum semantic search engine to find out whether quantum computing could take work away from datacentres. It can compute the similarity between two sentences. It computes exactly the number numpy computes, about two billion times more slowly, on a circuit that cannot finish a single shot.

Every number on this page was measured on one desktop machine, or is cited to a source. Nothing is asserted from memory. The code that produces all of it is in the repository.

What you are looking at

Entanglement, drawn

A quantum state of n qubits is 2n complex numbers. Both plates below are the full amplitude vector of a 12-qubit state - 4,096 complex numbers - with brightness for magnitude and hue for phase. The low six qubits index the horizontal axis, the high six index the vertical.

That mapping is the whole trick. A state that factorises across that split is an outer product, so it draws as a plaid: a landscape made of one row profile times one column profile. An entangled state does not factorise, and the plaid breaks.

The thesis under test

Where quantum and language actually touch

The strong claim - that quantum machines will run language models and make datacentres redundant - fails on one fact. Loading an arbitrary d-dimensional vector into amplitudes costs O(d) gates, so the classical work is finished before the quantum part starts. There is no quantum RAM anywhere, and dense arithmetic over unstructured data is not on the list of things quantum is provably good at.

But there is one genuine point of contact, and it is worth taking seriously. The swap test computes |⟨ψ|φ⟩|2 natively - and inner products between embeddings are precisely what vector search is. Retrieval is one of the largest workloads in any datacentre. If a quantum device computed that primitive natively and cheaply, the argument would have legs.

So I built it: real sentence embeddings, real amplitude encoding, a real swap test, and a retrieval benchmark on labelled documents. Five findings, in the order they have to be taken.

01

The quantum kernel is bit-identical to cosine squared

Encode an embedding as amplitudes and ⟨ψ(u)|ψ(v)⟩ is just u·v. Across six register widths on 700 held-out documents, the largest difference between the swap test's output and classical cosine2 was 0.00e+00. Not close - equal, in floating point. The swap test does not find a richer notion of similarity than a dot product. It finds the same one.

02

Its only measurable difference from cosine is a loss

The swap test returns the squared magnitude, which discards the sign of the inner product. Two documents pointing in opposite directions score as maximally similar. That is not an edge case: 42% of document pairs in this corpus have a negative cosine, and squaring the sign away costs 0.0063 mean precision@10. The quantum measurement is strictly lossier than the classical operation it replaces.

03

The phase kernel loses, and tuning tries to switch it off

Adding an IQP phase channel does produce a genuinely non-classical similarity function. It beat the equal-width classical baseline at 0 of 6 widths, with its scale parameter tuned in its favour at every width. The revealing part is which scale the search chose: the smallest offered, five times out of six. The tuner was trying to turn the quantum-specific term down to nothing.

04

The bill is nine orders of magnitude, and it is mostly the loading

One inner product to ±0.001 needs 750,000 shots, because the estimator's error falls as 1/√S - each decimal place costs a hundredfold more runs, and every run re-loads both vectors from scratch, since measurement destroys them. Of the 550 two-qubit gates in one shot, 494 are state preparation and 56 are the swap test everyone quotes.

05

On today's hardware it does not run at all

At the median two-qubit error rate of a current superconducting processor, this circuit accumulates 1.57 expected two-qubit errors per shot. Not per experiment - per single repetition, of which it needs 750,000. The honest verdict is not that quantum vector search is slow. It is that without full error correction, it does not produce an answer.

Evidence · retrieval

Nearest-neighbour retrieval on 20 Newsgroups

Each of 700 held-out documents is a query; a hit is a retrieved document from the same newsgroup. PCA is fitted on a disjoint 700-document training split, so the projection never sees what it is scored on. The challenger gets its hyperparameter tuned; the baselines have none.

What one similarity costs

Between two 256-dimensional vectors, to three decimal places. The quantum side is derived from gate counts measured by transpiling the real circuit to a superconducting basis, priced at a published gate duration. The classical side is wall-clock on the same desk.

The steelman

Which of these costs could actually go away

A debunk that stops here is not useful. The three costs behave completely differently under progress, and only one of them is truly load-bearing.

  • State preparation is the real barrier, and it is not an engineering detail. It only disappears if quantum RAM is invented, or if the data is generated by a quantum process rather than loaded from a disk. That second clause is the genuinely interesting frontier: quantum machines are not promising for data you already have, they are promising for data that is natively quantum - molecules, materials, lattice models.
  • Shot noise is the Born rule, not a defect. Amplitude estimation improves it quadratically, from 1/ε2 to 1/ε, which would turn 750,000 shots into roughly 1,000 - but it needs deep coherent circuits, so it trades a sampling cost for an error-correction cost.
  • The refrigerator is the one that genuinely amortises. Twenty-five to fifty kilowatts sounds fatal, and for one inner product it is. But that draw is roughly fixed as qubit count scales, which is the real reason anyone believes this wins eventually. It is a floor, not a marginal cost.

So the honest summary is narrow and worth stating precisely: quantum computing will not take vector search away from datacentres, because the loading dominates and the measurement is lossier than the arithmetic it replaces. The energy argument that actually threatens the datacentre is a different one - thermodynamic sampling, where generation is treated as a physical process rather than a computation. That is not quantum, and it is a separate investigation.

Reproduce it

Method

Embeddings from all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (384-d). Amplitude encoding via PCA to 2n dimensions, fitted on the training split only. Gate counts from Qiskit's own StatePreparation, transpiled at optimisation level 3 to an rz / sx / x / cx basis. Shot-count scaling derived from binomial variance and confirmed empirically by sampling the circuit on Aer.

python verify.py 12           # gate and circuit physics

python shotnoise.py           # 1/sqrt(S) measured, not assumed
python benchmark.py           # the retrieval table above

Cited, not measured: two-qubit gate duration 68 ns and median two-qubit error 2.848×10-3 for IBM Heron R2 (arXiv:2410.00916); dilution refrigerator draw 25-50 kW continuous. Everything else on this page came off one desktop with two consumer GPUs and is regenerated by the commands above.

Limits worth naming. One corpus, one embedding model, one phase-map family. A different feature map might do better than the IQP form tested here - the result that generalises is finding 01, the kernel identity, which is algebraic and holds regardless of encoding. The cost figures assume serial gate execution and no error correction; both would change under a fault-tolerant architecture, in opposite directions.
